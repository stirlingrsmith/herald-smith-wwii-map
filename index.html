Herald Thomas Smith — from Sapper to Rifleman: his route, 1942–1946

What is a sapper? A sapper is a soldier of the engineers — the army’s builders and demolishers, who put up bridges, clear mines and obstacles, and build the roads the rest of the army moves on. In the Royal Canadian Engineers, Sapper is also a rank: the engineers’ equivalent of a private.

Herald Thomas Smith (M-35398), born in Raymond, Alberta, enlisted in Calgary on 10 March 1942. His discharge certificate names the 9th Field Company, but his full Record of Service — preserved in the family papers — tells a different story: he was with the 9th for about three weeks, in Canada, and never served with it overseas. This page follows his own trail: engineer training in Canada and England, the 33rd Field Company in Normandy, two hospitals, a transfer to the infantry, and finally the Regina Rifle Regiment — his fighting unit from November 1944 to the end of the war.

Why the discharge certificate can mislead. The certificate names the 9th Field Company — the unit Smith joined on enlistment — but the service record (M.F.M. 1, three pages, and the Service and Casualty Form, M.F.M. 4(a) Part II, two pages) shows he was struck off the 9th on 10 April 1942, before it ever left Canada, and spent the rest of the war in other units. Everything below follows the record, line by line; where a place isn’t on the record, the stop says so plainly rather than guessing.

His war, in one line: basic training at Grande Prairie, engineer training at Chilliwack, overseas to the 1st Canadian Engineer Reinforcement Unit at Aldershot in September 1943, the 33rd Field Company in Normandy by July 1944, hospital and convalescence that summer, remustered to the infantry in September 1944, across to Belgium in October, and taken on strength of the Regina Rifle Regiment — 7th Canadian Infantry Brigade, 3rd Canadian Infantry Division — on 3 November 1944.

Medals — and what they confirm

  • 1939–45 Star — its award is entered on his Service and Casualty Form.
  • France and Germany Star — also entered on the form; service in France, Belgium, Holland and Germany, 6 Jun 1944–8 May 1945. His Normandy service with the 33rd Field Company and his campaign with the Regina Rifles both fall inside it.
  • Defence Medal — entered on the form as well.
  • Canadian Volunteer Service Medal with Clasp — the clasp marks overseas service.
  • War Medal 1939–45

All five fit his record: the France and Germany Star is earned twice over — once as a Sapper in Normandy in July 1944, and again as a Rifleman from Belgium to VE Day.

Unit route ≠ personal route. From November 1944 the stops follow the Regina Rifle Regiment, and Smith was on its strength the whole time. But a battalion is hundreds of men: he could have been on leave, on a course, or briefly in a dressing station at a given moment without it appearing on these forms. His full service file at Library and Archives Canada would settle his personal dates.

After the war

Everyone in Cardston knew him as Bud. He married Jean Burton in 1949. After coming home he was in the trucking business for several years, then went into the family business, Spencer’s Hardware, where he stayed until he retired in September 1982.

The Legion was a large part of his life: he was president of the Cardston Branch of the Canadian Legion No. 170 for two terms, 1957–1958, and served two terms as Zone Commander for the Canadian Legion. He sat on Cardston Town Council for two terms, 1964–1970, was chairman of the Police Commission, and worked on Public Works and the Finance Committee. He was also director of the Emergency Measures Organization from 1964 to 1970.

Shortly before his death, Bud shared his war experiences and memories with The Cardston Chronicle for a Special Remembrance Day feature. He said the most hazardous part of his service was holding the Nijmegen Bridge — and that he would serve his country again:

“I would go again, but knowing there had to be a better way...”
— Herald (Bud) Smith, in The Cardston Chronicle’s Remembrance Day feature, November 1983

Herald (Bud) Smith died suddenly in Cardston on Friday, November 18, 1983. He was survived by his wife Jean, one daughter and two sons. His funeral was held at the Temple Street Chapel.

What’s settled — and what still isn’t

1. The rank question — settled

The Part II rank column reads Spr (Sapper) the whole time he was in the Engineers, switches to Pte when he moves to the infantry in September 1944, and to Rfl (Rifleman) with the Regina Rifles — a rifle regiment, where a private is a Rifleman. That is why the discharge certificate says Rifleman: it reflects his last unit, not a clerical error.

2. The 9th Field Company — three weeks, in Canada

He was posted to it on enlistment, 10 March 1942, and struck off on transfer to Grande Prairie on 10 April 1942. He never served with it in Europe. The discharge certificate still names “9th Field Coy” because that was the unit he joined — not the one he fought with.

3. “38 Fd” or 33rd Field Company?

His Part II form’s posting line of 18 March 1944 names the reporting unit “38 Fd,” while the M.F.M. 1 says 33rd Field Company twice. No Canadian 38th Field Company RCE existed in the UK in March 1944 — every “38th Field Company” of the period is British Royal Engineers. It is most likely a clerk’s slip for the 33rd; a one-day posting to a 38th can’t be entirely ruled out, so this page traces the 33rd.

4. Rocky Mountain Rangers — not the Royal Montreal Regiment

The handwritten “Rocky Mtn R.” on the form is easy to misread as the Royal Montreal Regiment. The record says the Rocky Mountain Rangers (1st Battalion), an infantry regiment from Kamloops, BC, then in England with the 13th Canadian Infantry Brigade as a training and reinforcement battalion, being broken up to reinforce under-strength units. He was with it 2 September – 12 October 1944, in England — a holding posting on the way to the front, not a fighting one.

5. Placeholder pins — read the notes

The record does not give the UK stations of the 2nd Road Construction Company, the 33rd Field Company, or the Rocky Mountain Rangers (whose station is recorded only as “A.41”), nor the site of 17 Canadian General Hospital, the exact X-4 camp in Belgium, or the regiment’s occupation station in 1945. Those pins sit at the nearest known point and say so. Nothing on this map presents an inferred place as a recorded one.

6. What would fill the remaining gaps

His full service file at Library and Archives Canada, plus the war diaries: the 33rd Field Company (LAC reels T-18591 and T-18592), the Regina Rifle Regiment (Nov 1944 – Oct 1945), the Rocky Mountain Rangers and 13th Canadian Infantry Brigade, and the 17th and 18th Canadian General Hospitals. Together they could pin down the unknown stations, the 33rd’s Normandy landing date, and his day-to-day whereabouts.
